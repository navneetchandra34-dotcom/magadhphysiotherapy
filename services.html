CONDITIONS & SERVICES

Support for the things
you want to do again.

Physiotherapy may help people manage selected symptoms, improve function and work towards activity goals. The right approach depends on an individual assessment and any medical precautions.

This page describes areas where physiotherapy may be considered; it does not confirm a diagnosis or guarantee that a specific treatment is suitable for every person.

01 · MUSCULOSKELETAL

Back & neck pain

Back or neck discomfort can affect sitting, sleeping, lifting, work and daily activity. A physiotherapy assessment may explore movement, strength, functional tolerance, symptom behaviour and relevant medical history.

Care may focus on

  • Understanding movements and activities that affect symptoms.
  • Appropriate mobility, strengthening and graded activity exercises.
  • Practical advice about pacing, posture variation and daily tasks.
  • Recognising symptoms that require medical review rather than routine rehabilitation.

Conditions such as sciatica or suspected disc-related problems need appropriate assessment. New or worsening weakness, saddle-area numbness, or loss of bladder/bowel control requires urgent medical evaluation.

02 · JOINTS & ORTHOPAEDIC REHABILITATION

Knee, shoulder and other joint conditions

Joint pain may arise from several causes, including injury, arthritis, overuse or recovery after surgery. A plan should reflect the diagnosis, symptoms, mobility and medical advice.

Rehabilitation goals may include

  • Maintaining or improving comfortable range of movement.
  • Building strength and tolerance for everyday activities.
  • Practising balance, walking and functional tasks where appropriate.
  • Supporting a graded return to activity after an injury or procedure.

Seek medical evaluation for a hot, red, markedly swollen joint with fever, inability to bear weight after injury, or sudden severe symptoms.

03 · NEUROLOGICAL REHABILITATION

Paralysis, stroke and neurological conditions

After a stroke or other neurological event, rehabilitation goals may involve safe movement, sitting and standing balance, transfers, walking practice, strength, coordination and participation in daily activities. The plan must be tailored to the person's medical status and abilities.

What a goal-led plan can involve

  • Identifying meaningful functional goals with the patient and, where appropriate, caregivers.
  • Practising safe movement and task-specific activities.
  • Reviewing mobility aids, falls risk and home safety needs when relevant.
  • Coordinating with the wider healthcare team when appropriate.

Sudden facial droop, new weakness or numbness, speech difficulty, sudden vision changes or loss of balance may signal a stroke. Call emergency services immediately; do not wait for a physiotherapy appointment.

04 · SPORTS & ACTIVITY

Sports injuries and soft-tissue problems

Sprains, strains and activity-related pain can affect training and confidence. The timing and intensity of rehabilitation should depend on the type and severity of injury, assessment findings and any required medical investigations.

  • Gradual mobility and strength progression where appropriate.
  • Balance, coordination and movement-control exercises.
  • Activity modification and staged return-to-sport planning.
  • Guidance on when symptoms suggest a need for further assessment.
05 · MOBILITY & HEALTHY AGEING

Strength, balance and mobility for older adults

Changes in strength, balance or confidence can make daily activities more difficult. An appropriate programme may work on safe transfers, walking, lower-limb strength, balance and practical strategies for daily routines.

Exercise selection should account for falls history, cardiovascular and other health conditions, pain, medication effects and the person's abilities. Ask the clinician whether family or caregiver involvement may be helpful.

06 · RECOVERY & FUNCTION

Post-surgical and general rehabilitation

Rehabilitation after surgery or illness should follow the treating medical team's precautions and milestones. The plan may include movement, strength, functional retraining and gradual return to daily tasks when clinically appropriate.

Bring discharge notes, operation details and the treating clinician's instructions to help guide safe progression.

What happens next?

Contact the clinic to describe your main concern and ask about appointment availability. Bring relevant medical reports and tell the clinician about current medicines, prior surgery, other health conditions and any new or worsening symptoms.
